Almost everything written about this starts from the assumption that you need
somebody to find you a training place. You do not. The databases are public,
free, and open from Nepal. What you need is a plan, a lot of applications, and
an employer who is willing to deal with a visa.

**One word first, because it matters.** An *Ausbildungsplatz* is a **training
place**, and it is neither quite a job nor quite a course. You sign a contract
with a German company, you are paid every month while you learn, you spend part
of each week in a vocational school, and at the end you hold a qualification the
German state recognises. This page says "training place" throughout.
*Ausbildungsplatz* is the word to type into the German search boxes.

## Places are not the scarce thing

On **21 August 2026** the Federal Employment Agency's own job board listed
**165,002 training places** across Germany — 155,530 of them ordinary company
training places, the rest dual study programmes. Anyone can search it. There is
no login, no fee, and no agent between you and the listing.

So when you are told that training places are scarce and that is why the fee is
16 lakh, the first half of that sentence is not true.

The scarce thing is different, and nobody advertises it: **an employer prepared
to hire someone who is not yet in Germany.** A small company with four staff can
fill its vacancy with a local applicant in three weeks. Taking you means
paperwork it has never done, a chamber registration, and a wait of months. Most
will not. Some will. Your job is to find the ones who will, and there is no
shortcut except volume.

## You can apply for any Ausbildung — there is no approved list

This is worth stating plainly, because the opposite is constantly implied.

The residence permit for vocational training is **§ 16a AufenthG**, and it names
no trades at all. There is no list of approved occupations, no
shortage-occupation requirement, and no rule that applicants from Nepal must go
into care work or hospitality. What the law asks is that the Federal Employment
Agency consents to your particular contract under § 39 — a check on that
employer and those terms, not on the trade you chose.

So if you want to train as a joiner, a mechatronics technician, a baker, a
warehouse logistics specialist or a hairdresser, the law is not what stands in
your way. Anyone who tells you only certain trades are open to Nepalis should be
asked where they read that.

Three more things sit in the same short section of the law, and almost nobody is
told them:

- **A German course to prepare for the training is part of the purpose.**
  § 16a says the purpose *"umfasst auch den Besuch eines Deutschsprachkurses zur
  Vorbereitung auf die Berufsausbildung"*. That is the law. It does not change
  what the embassy in Kathmandu asks you to hand over at the counter — [see the
  language page](/exam-slots/) — but it is there.
- **You may work up to 20 hours a week** in something unconnected to the
  training, and no more than that.
- **If the training place collapses through no fault of your own**, you must be
  given **up to six months** to find another one before the permit is taken
  away. You are not put on a plane the week a company goes under.

### And there is no minimum school certificate either

The same is true of your schooling, and it surprises people. The Federal
Employment Agency states it plainly: *"Rechtlich gesehen brauchst du keinen
bestimmten Schulabschluss für deine duale Ausbildung."* Legally you need no
particular school certificate for a dual Ausbildung. Employers decide for
themselves what they will ask for — *"Die Betriebe dürfen selbst entscheiden,
welchen Abschluss die Bewerberinnen und Bewerber mitbringen müssen."*

What the embassy in Kathmandu asks you to hand over is your
**Schulabschlusszeugnis mit den Noten** — your school leaving certificate, with
the grades on it. It asks for the certificate you have. It names no minimum
level, and for this visa it asks for no equivalence assessment: no anabin entry,
no Zeugnisbewertung. The skilled-worker employment visa is different and does
require one. Do not read across from that.

So **Class 10 on its own does not shut the door.** Where a German employer states
a minimum at all, it is usually a Hauptschulabschluss — nine years of school. The
SEE sits at the end of Grade 10.

Three honest limits on that:

- **Schulische Ausbildungen are a different thing.** The school-based trainings,
  as opposed to the dual ones inside a company, commonly do ask for a mittlerer
  Abschluss. Your choice is wide, not unlimited.
- **Nursing has a rule written into the statute.** § 11 PflBG wants a mittlerer
  Schulabschluss — but it accepts as an alternative *"der erfolgreiche Abschluss
  einer sonstigen zehnjährigen allgemeinen Schulbildung"*, any other ten-year
  general schooling. Ten years is what the SEE is. Whether a particular German
  state authority counts a Nepali SEE that way is **their decision, and we have
  not seen it answered.** Ask before you build a plan on it.
- **Nobody has published how Kathmandu treats a Class 10 file in practice.** A
  checklist that names no minimum is not the same as a post that accepts
  anything.

None of this is likely to be the thing standing between you and a place. **B1
German and an exam date are** — and that is a queue, not a certificate.

One thing about the certificate does matter to an employer, though: **which board
issued it.** The embassy and the employer work from different lists, and the
employers we have read name Goethe, telc and ÖSD. [Which providers employers
actually name](/exam-slots/).

### But some trades are much easier to get into than others

Both halves of this are true and you need both. **The law lets you apply for
anything. The odds are not the same everywhere.**

In 2024 Germany had **69,405 training places that nobody took** — 12.8% of
everything employers offered. In the crafts (*Handwerk*) it was worse: around
19,000 unfilled places, **18%** of what craft firms advertised. In individual
trades it goes higher again. Pipe-laying (*Rohrleitungsbauer*) left 145 of its
405 places empty, **35.8%**.

Two facts from the same report are worth more to you than any list of trades:

- **More than half of those unfilled places — 38,611 of them, 55.6% — asked only
  for a *Hauptschulabschluss***, the most basic German school-leaving
  certificate. If you have been told your schooling is not good enough for
  Germany, that is where the evidence points.
- **35% of the firms that could not fill a place received no application at
  all.** Not a weak one. None. You are not an imposition on these employers.

**So why is there no list of shortage occupations on this page?** Two reasons,
and they are the reasons this whole site exists.

It would rot. The trades shift every year, and a page that quietly goes out of
date is worse than no page — you would plan around a number we stopped checking.
And a list is exactly how people get steered. Telling you that your realistic
options are care work and hospitality is what an agent does. We are not going to
publish a politer version of the same thing.

There is also a trap inside the numbers. Retail (*Kauffrau/Kaufmann im
Einzelhandel*) left 31.8% of its training places unfilled, which looks like an
open door — and yet 2,501 people who wanted to train in retail ended that year
with nothing. Unfilled and easy are not the same thing. Employers and applicants
manage to miss each other inside a single trade.

**What to do instead of reading a list.** Search the
[Jobsuche](https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/jobsuche/) for the trade you actually
want, and see how many vacancies are still open late in the season. That is this
year's answer rather than last year's, it is free, and you can read it yourself
in ten minutes. Then apply widely. If your first choice turns out to be crowded,
the construction, metal, food and craft trades are where the empty places have
been for years — and a willingness to go somewhere small and unglamorous is
worth more than any shortage list.

Every regional chamber — the **IHK** for commercial and industrial trades, the
**Handwerkskammer** for crafts — also runs its own vacancy exchange for its
area. Search for the chamber's name and the city. We are deliberately not
printing a single national chamber link here: the address most guides still give
(*ihk-lehrstellenboerse.de*) now redirects to a campaign page with no search on
it at all. We checked again on 23 August 2026. It is still dead.

<div class="warn">

**One of these is not what it looks like.** The Federal Employment Agency also
runs a page called **Ausbildungssuche**, at *web.arbeitsagentur.de/ausbildungssuche*,
and most guides list it as a place to find vacancies. It is not one. It is a
directory of **courses and training providers** — vocational schools,
rehabilitation training centres, publicly funded programmes — and the entries
name a *Bildungsanbieter*, not an employer offering you a contract.

For someone applying from Nepal that distinction decides everything. No employer
means no training contract, no contract means no § 39 consent, and no consent
means no visa. Search the **Jobsuche** instead.

</div>

The free databases are all in German. That is not an accident of design, and
getting through them is the first honest test of whether your German is where you
think it is.

**Searching more boards is not the lever you think it is.** The agency's board
and the commercial ones list largely the same employers. What decides your
outcome is not how many sites you searched but how many applications you sent,
and whether the employer is willing to take on a visa. Two databases and three
hundred applications beats six databases and thirty.

<div class="warn">

**Placement by the Federal Employment Agency is free. They have said so in
writing.** In August 2026 its international division, the ZAV, told us:

> Die Vermittlung durch die Bundesagentur für Arbeit ist für Bewerberinnen und
> Bewerber stets kostenfrei… Keinesfalls sollten Interessierte Geld an private
> Mittelsmänner zahlen, die mit angeblich garantierten Visa oder Programmplätzen
> werben.

Placement through the Federal Employment Agency is always free of charge for
applicants, and under no circumstances should people pay money to private
middlemen advertising supposedly guaranteed visas or programme places.

</div>

## What a German application contains

German employers expect a specific set of documents, in a specific order. This
is the Federal Employment Agency's own list:

1. **Anschreiben** — the covering letter. One page.
2. **Deckblatt** — a cover sheet. Optional; the agency says plainly it is *"kein
   Pflichtbestandteil"*.
3. **Lebenslauf** — the CV, in the German tabular style. The agency calls it
   *"das Herzstück deiner Bewerbung"* — the heart of your application.
4. **Zeugnisse** — your school certificates. The agency says the **last two**,
   plus anything else relevant, with translations.
5. Your **language certificate**, or the date you are booked to sit the exam.

Two things matter more than polish. Write it in German — an application in
English, for a German-language training place, answers a question the employer
was about to ask, and answers it badly. And say plainly, near the start, that
you are applying from Nepal and will need a visa. Hiding it wastes your time and
theirs; the ones who would have said yes will still say yes.

## The Lebenslauf: what goes in, and what must stay out

A German CV is a **tabellarischer Lebenslauf** — a table, not an essay. There is a **[free template](/templates/)** you can copy into your own Google Drive, fill in on your phone, and share with your teacher for correction. Dates in
a column on the right, facts on the left, most recent first. Two pages at the
absolute most, and one is better when you are eighteen.

**One short summary at the top is worth having.** A *Kurzprofil* or *Profil* —
two or three factual sentences saying who you are, which trade you want, your
German level and when you are free — has become normal in German CVs, and one
survey of hiring decision-makers found a majority value it. It is worth most to
exactly the person reading this: someone with little work history, whose dates
cannot speak for themselves.

What to avoid is the other thing — a paragraph of adjectives about how motivated
and hard-working you are, or a block that repeats in sentences what the sections
below already say in dates. Facts, not adjectives, and never twice.

**What belongs in it**

- Name, address, telephone number with the country code, email address.
- Date and place of birth. In Germany this is normal and expected — unlike in
  Britain or America.
- Schooling, with dates and the qualification obtained.
- Any work, internships, family business, volunteering. Real work counts even
  if it was unpaid or informal, and even if it was for a relative. Say what you
  actually did.
- Languages, each with an honest level: Nepali (mother tongue), English (B2),
  German (A2, B1 exam booked for March).
- Computer skills, driving licence, anything concrete.
- Two or three genuine interests, briefly.

<div class="warn">

**What must not go in — and this is where applications from South Asia usually
give themselves away.** A CV that looks completely normal in Kathmandu can look
wrong in Germany, for a specific legal reason. Under the
**Allgemeines Gleichbehandlungsgesetz**, Germany's equal treatment law, an
employer is not allowed to select on these grounds, so putting them in your CV
asks them to do something unlawful. It reads as though you do not know how
things work.

**Leave all of this out:**

- **Marital status** — no *ledig*, no *verheiratet*, no "single".
- **Your father's name or occupation.** Standard on a Nepali CV. Not on a German one.
- **Religion or caste.** Never.
- **Number of children**, or anything about your family situation.
- **Health, disability or blood group.**
- **Passport, citizenship or ID numbers.** Nationality alone is fine and can be
  useful here, since the employer needs to know a visa is involved — but never
  the document numbers.
- **Your exact height, weight or a full-length photograph.**
- **Percentage marks and class rank from every year of school.** Give the
  qualification and the year. If a grade is genuinely good, say so once.

None of this makes you a weaker candidate. It is simply not what the document is
for, and removing it makes room for what is.

</div>

**The photograph.** You will get contradictory advice, so here is the actual
position. It is **not required** — the Federal Employment Agency says
*"ein Bewerbungsfoto ist zwar keine Pflicht mehr"* — and an employer may not
demand one. But the same sentence continues: *"jedoch in vielen Bränchen
weiterhin üblich"* — still usual in many industries. So it is your choice, and
either choice is normal.

If you include one, it must be a proper photograph: head and shoulders, plain
background, ordinary clothes, looking at the camera. **Not a selfie, and not
your passport photo.** Both are specifically warned against, and both are what
people send.

**A sixty-second video is the thing almost nobody sends, and it answers the
employer's real worry.** Video applications are still relatively new in Germany
and are treated as a supplement rather than a replacement — the Federal
Employment Agency has its own guidance on them. For someone applying from
Nepal they do a specific job: an employer who has never met you is quietly
wondering whether you can really hold a conversation in German. A minute of you
saying who you are and why this trade settles it in a way no certificate can.

Record it in German, at whatever level you are actually at. Put it on Google
Drive or YouTube as **unlisted**, and paste the link into your CV. If it is bad,
do not send it — no video is much better than a careless one. The template has
a line for the link.

## The Anschreiben: the letter that decides it

The CV says what you have done. The letter says why this employer, and it is the
part almost everybody wastes.

The Federal Employment Agency's advice is blunt: do not send the same letter to
everybody. Open with something about **that company** which shows you looked at
it, then explain *"warum ich denn diese Ausbildung absolvieren möchte"* — why
you want this particular training.

A structure that works, four short paragraphs on one page:

1. **Why them.** One or two sentences proving you read their website. What they
   make, where they are, how long they have trained apprentices. Never "Sehr
   geehrte Damen und Herren, I am writing to apply for any available position."
2. **Why this trade.** What draws you to it, and anything you have actually done
   that touches it — helping in a workshop, a family shop, a kitchen, a farm.
   Concrete beats enthusiastic.
3. **Where your German is.** State your level, name the exam and the date, and
   say what you are doing between now and then. An employer who knows you sit
   B1 in March can plan around it. One who is left guessing will not.
4. **The visa, in plain words.** You are in Nepal, you will need a visa, and
   there is a faster route their own Ausländerbehörde runs which costs them
   €411. Offer them [the page that explains it](/visa/). This paragraph is the
   single most useful thing in the letter, because it turns "complicated" into
   "a thing with a procedure".

Close with **Mit freundlichen Grüßen** and your name.

**Do not have an AI write it for you in German you cannot read.** German
employers are already seeing a lot of that, and it collapses at the first phone
call, which is exactly the moment you cannot afford it to. Write it yourself and
have your teacher correct it. A B1 letter that is plainly yours is worth far more
than a C1 letter that is plainly not.

## The Anlagen: send one file, not ten

*Anlagen* means the attachments. Getting this wrong is the commonest way a
perfectly good application annoys the person opening it.

**Send one PDF.** Not ten. Not a folder, not a zip, not eight photographs and a
Word file. For an online or email application it is normal in Germany to combine
everything into a single PDF, in this order:

1. **Anschreiben** — the covering letter
2. **Lebenslauf** — the CV
3. **Zeugnisse** — your school certificates, **most recent first**
4. **Language certificate**, or the confirmation that you are booked for the exam
5. Any reference or internship confirmation you actually have

**Keep it small.** Aim for **under 3 MB**, and treat 5 MB as the hard ceiling —
many company mailboxes reject more, and some application forms will not accept
it. If a company states its own limit, that limit wins.

**Name the file so a stranger can file it.** `Bewerbung_Thapa_Bishal.pdf` is
right. `IMG_20260822_final2.pdf` is not. Put your surname in it: the person
receiving it has forty others.

### What does not go in — and this matters

<div class="warn">

**Do not send a copy of your passport.** Not with the application. An employer
deciding whether to interview you does not need your identity documents, and has
no business asking for them at this stage. There is a later point — a signed
contract, the visa step — where a passport copy is genuinely required, and that
is when it goes.

A passport scan sitting in a stranger's inbox is the single most useful document
for anyone committing fraud in your name. Once you have sent it you cannot get it
back, and you will never know where it went.

</div>

Also leave out, for the same reasons the section above explains:

- **Citizenship certificate, birth certificate, marriage certificate.** Not
  needed to decide whether to train you.
- **Bank statements or proof of funds.** Never. Nobody legitimate asks an
  apprentice for these.
- **Police clearance or medical certificates**, unless the employer has
  specifically asked and explained why.
- **Your marks from every year of school.** The last two certificates are what
  the Federal Employment Agency's own guidance asks for.
- **More photographs of yourself.** One in the CV, at most. No full-length shots.

If an employer or an agent asks for your passport before there is a contract, that
is a reasonable moment to ask **why**, in writing, and to say you will provide it
at the visa stage. An honest employer will not mind. [What you should pay for, and
what you should not](/no-fees/).

### How to make one PDF on a phone

The mistake is to make several PDFs and then try to join them together. On a
phone that is genuinely hard, and the websites that offer to do it want you to
upload your personal documents to a stranger's server.

**Do it the other way round: put everything into one document first, then export
that document once.** Your letter and CV are already Google Docs, so the whole
application can be assembled inside one of them.

**Step by step, on the phone:**

1. Open your **Anschreiben** — your own copy in your Drive.
2. Go to the very end of it. Tap **+**, then **Page break**.
3. Open your **Lebenslauf**, select everything, copy it, come back to the
   Anschreiben and paste it after that break. Your letter and CV are now one
   document, in the right order.
4. Add another **Page break**, then tap **+** → **Image** and insert your
   certificates, one after another.
5. Export the whole thing: the **⋮** menu → **Share & export** → **Save As** →
   **PDF Document**. Then tap the download icon to get it onto your phone.

That is one PDF, in the correct order, made without a printer, without a scanner
and without uploading anything to a website you do not know.

**Keep the original two documents as they are.** Do the joining in a *copy*, so
your Lebenslauf stays a clean separate document you can edit and reuse for the
next employer.

**Your certificates never change, so photograph them once.** Get them into a
document of their own, get them looking right, and leave it alone. For every
application after the first, joining up the file is a two-minute job: copy the
letter, paste the CV under it, paste the certificates under that, export.

**If the employer's own form asks for separate files, follow the form.** Some
online application systems have a slot for the Anschreiben, a slot for the
Lebenslauf and a slot for the attachments. When that happens, upload them
separately — the one-file rule is for applications you send by email, and the
employer's own instructions always win.

### Getting a paper certificate into the phone

Your school certificates are on paper, so they have to become pictures before you
can put them in the document.

**If you have a scanning app**, use it — Google Drive, Microsoft Lens, Adobe
Scan and the iPhone Files app all have one. They straighten the page, sharpen the
text and remove the shadow of your hand. Where the app offers to share the result
as an **image** rather than a PDF, choose the image, because that is what you can
insert into the document.

**If you do not, a careful photograph is fine.** Careless ones are the problem,
not photographs. So:

- Put the certificate on a **flat surface**, not on your lap.
- Use **daylight**, near a window, and do not use the flash.
- Hold the phone **directly above** the page, not at an angle, so the edges stay
  straight.
- Make sure **your own shadow is not across the page**.
- **Crop** to the edges of the paper before you insert it.

**Then check the size.** Photographs are heavy and a PDF full of them can easily
pass 5 MB. If yours does, retake the pictures with a lower camera quality
setting, or scan in black and white. A certificate has to be *readable*, not
beautiful.

**If you can get to a computer** — at an institute, a library, a friend's house —
open your document in a browser and use **File → Download → PDF Document**. It is
the same result and slightly less fiddly.

## Work backwards from the exam, not forwards from today

This is where most plans break. The training year normally starts in **August or
September**. The Federal Employment Agency tells German school leavers to start
looking *"eineinhalb Jahre vor Beginn der Ausbildung"* — **eighteen months
ahead**. Chambers advise employers to recruit roughly six to eight months ahead,
and to allow four to six months between signing a contract and the trainee
actually arriving.

**Missing September does not cost you a year.** This is worth being clear about,
because the fear of it pushes people into bad decisions. German chamber guidance
is explicit that *"grundsätzlich kann zu jedem Tag im Jahr eine Ausbildung
begonnen werden"* — in principle an Ausbildung can begin on any day of the year.
What a late start changes is which examination sitting you land in: begin by
**1 October** and you can still sit the summer final exam after three years;
begin later and you are scheduled for the winter sitting instead, which pushes
your finish date by a few months rather than a year. The other real cost is
vocational school, which runs on an annual cycle, so a late starter joins a class
already under way and has ground to make up.

So it is a reason to move quickly, not a reason to give up on the year — and it
is a reason to talk to your employer early, because [the start date can be
moved by agreement](/visa-late/) and the contract cannot be backdated.

Those are the timescales for someone who is already in Germany, already speaks
German, and does not need a visa.

Now add the two things that are specific to Nepal. Your B1 certificate must be
in hand **when you apply for the visa**, not later — and the exam queue in
Kathmandu is measured in months, not weeks. Then the visa itself.

So the sequence that works is: **exam date first, then applications, then
contract, then visa.** People who apply for places before they have any route to
a certificate are the ones who end up with a signed contract and no visa.

## How to tell whether an employer will really sponsor you

**Not in your first email.** These are not screening questions to put to a
company that has never heard of you — an application that opens with a list of
demands about visa procedure gets deleted. Wait until somebody has written back,
or invited you to talk. Once a company has shown real interest, these become
reasonable and even welcome, because they are the questions the employer is
about to have to answer anyway.

- **Have you employed a trainee from outside the EU before?** The answer tells
  you almost everything. A company that has done it once will do it again.
- **Will you apply for the *beschleunigtes Fachkräfteverfahren* under § 81a?**
  This is the single most valuable thing an employer can do for you, it costs
  them €411, and most have never heard of it. [The detail is
  here](/visa/) — send them that page.
- **Can the contract be made conditional on the visa being issued?**
  [Ask before you sign](/visa-late/), not after.

An employer who will not answer these once they are interested is not
necessarily dishonest. But they are telling you they have not thought about the
visa, and that is the part that will decide whether you actually go.

## If someone offers to do all this for a fee

They are offering to do what is on this page. Search a free database, write a
letter, and send an email. That is worth something — but it is worth what a
week of skilled work is worth, itemised, not seven to sixteen lakh.
[What you should pay, and what you should not](/no-fees/).

**On the § 81a procedure, be precise about what can and cannot be sold.** Nobody
can file it for you in your own name: the law lets the *employer* apply, *"in
Vollmacht des Ausländers"* — on your authority, not instead of it. But the same
paragraph then says *"Arbeitgeber können zur Durchführung des Verfahrens Dritte
bevollmächtigen"* — employers may appoint a third party to run the procedure for
them.

So a service that handles § 81a is lawful and can be genuinely useful. Two
things follow, and they are the whole point. **The employer appoints it, not
you** — if someone in Kathmandu offers to run § 81a on your behalf, they are
describing something the law does not provide. And under § 296a SGB III **the
employer pays for it.** A fee for arranging an Ausbildung may be taken from the
employer and from nobody else.
