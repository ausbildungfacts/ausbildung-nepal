Au Pair gets suggested to people in Nepal as an alternative to Ausbildung,
and it comes up most when the B1 exam queue starts to look impossible. That
advice is not mad. But the reason it is usually given is a bad one, and both
halves are worth understanding before you follow it.

## What an Au Pair placement actually is

These come from the German Embassy in Kathmandu's own Au-pair page, so they are
the rules that will actually be applied to you.

- You live with a German host family, which must have **at least one child** —
  childless couples cannot take an Au Pair
- **At least one parent in the household must be a German citizen**, and the
  family should speak German. This rules out a great many families who would
  otherwise be glad to have you, so check it before you fall in love with an
  advertisement
- **Between 6 and 12 months**, and you may only do it **once**. Even if you leave
  early, you cannot come back on a second Au Pair placement
- **At least 18** and **not older than 27** when the stay begins
- German at **A1** — much lower than the B1 an Ausbildung visa needs. But the
  certificate must come from **VHS Bhaktapur or the Goethe-Zentrum Kathmandu**
  and be **less than six months old**, so this does not put you outside the exam
  system, only lower down it
- You must also be able to **hold an A1 conversation with embassy staff** —
  understand their questions and answer freely. A certificate alone is not enough
- **€280 a month** pocket money, plus board and lodging
- Maximum 6 hours a day and 30 hours a week, with at least two days off a month
- The host family must contribute **€50 a month** towards your German course
- You must have **permanent residence in Nepal** to apply in Kathmandu, and
  appear **in person**. The visa fee is **€75** in rupees

So it is not a job and it is not training. It is a year of living in a German
household, at pocket-money rates, while your German improves faster than any
classroom could manage.

## Why it works as a route into an Ausbildung

This is the part nobody explains properly.

The restriction that stops private agencies recruiting people from Nepal into
German health and care work applies to **recruitment from Nepal**. It does not
follow you. Someone already living legally in Germany who applies to a
Pflegeschule or a care home on their own initiative is in a completely different
position from someone being placed there from Kathmandu.

And this is documented, not theoretical. Five Nepali women are currently in
Pflegeausbildung at a clinic in Osnabrück; the first two arrived in Germany as Au
Pairs, decided during that year that they wanted to train in care, and applied
directly. Another Nepali woman in Wedel introduced herself at a care home, did a
trial day, and was taken on.

So the sequence is: **Au Pair → a year of immersion and a much better German level
→ apply from inside Germany → Ausbildung.** It is slower than going straight in.
It also sidesteps two of the hardest obstacles at once — the B1 certificate queue
and the restriction on care-sector recruitment.

<div class="warn">

**But check the reason you are being given.** If someone is recommending Au Pair
because *"Ausbildung is too late this year"* or because the exam queue is
impossible, they are treating a symptom. Notice what that advice actually means:
you cannot get an exam slot, so you are being redirected onto a path that needs a
lower certificate.

That may genuinely be the right call for you. But it is a workaround for a
bottleneck, not a judgement about what suits you — and it is being given to girls
far more often than to boys.

</div>

## The thing that makes this advice worse than it looks

**Au Pair has the longest visa queue of all.**

The embassy in Kathmandu publishes an approximate wait of **8 to 12 months** for
Au Pair and FSJ visa appointments. And it told us directly that trainees usually
move through the waiting list *faster* than Au Pairs:

> Die Wartelisten treffen alle gleich. Jedoch geht es bei Auszubildenden meist
> etwas schneller als z.B. bei Au-Pairs.

So switching from Ausbildung to Au Pair to escape the exam queue can put you into
a **longer** visa queue. And the Au Pair route has no equivalent of the §81a
accelerated procedure, because that is something a German *employer* triggers —
a host family is not an employer in that sense. [The three-week route](/visa/)
is not available to you as an Au Pair.

Add it up honestly:

<div class="scroll">

| | Ausbildung | Au Pair first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| German needed to leave Nepal | B1 | A1 |
| Visa queue | Long, but faster than Au Pair | 8–12 months published |
| Accelerated §81a route | **Yes** — employer can get you an appointment in 3 weeks | No |
| Paid | ~€1,000/month, rising | €280/month pocket money |
| Ends with | A recognised German qualification | Nothing, on its own |
| Time to qualification | 3 years | 4 years |

</div>

## What it is genuinely good for

Set against all that, there are real cases where this is the better path:

- **Your German is at A1 or A2 and the B1 queue is hopeless.** A year of
  immersion in a German household will take you further than a year of waiting.
- **You are not sure what trade you want.** A year in Germany is a good way to
  find out before committing three years to one.
- **You want care work but your German is short of what the schools ask.** Read
  the next section before you decide this one applies to you.

## If care work is what you want, apply for care work

If care work is the reason you are considering Au Pair, this is the section that
matters, because the common belief here is wrong. **Au Pair is not a shortcut
into care.** It is a longer, worse-paid way to reach the same place.

**The recruitment restriction binds agencies, not you.** Nepal is on the World
Health Organization's health workforce safeguards list, and German law
(§ 38 BeschV) responds by prohibiting private *recruitment and placement* into
German health and care jobs from those countries. It regulates what an agency
may do. **It is not a rule about whether you may apply.** Someone from Nepal
applying on their own initiative is doing something different from being
recruited.

So the direct route exists: apply to a clinic or a Pflegeschule yourself, the
same way you would apply for any other Ausbildung. Compared with going via Au
Pair it is a year shorter, it pays a trainee wage instead of €280 a month
pocket money, and [the §81a accelerated procedure](/visa/) is available because
there is a real employer at the other end.

**The genuine obstacle is language, not law.** The Ausbildung visa needs B1.
Care schools commonly ask for **B2** — the university clinic in Ulm, which
actively takes applicants from abroad, wants a recognised B2 with **all four
modules passed at 85% or better**. That gap is real, and Kathmandu's exam queue
makes closing it slow.

**That is the honest case for Au Pair here.** Not that it is cleaner — it is
not. It is that a year of living in German can carry you from B1 to B2 faster
than waiting in Kathmandu can, if care work is genuinely what you want and the
language is what is stopping you.

<div class="warn">

**One thing we still cannot tell you.** Whether § 38 BeschV touches a Pflege
*Ausbildung*, as opposed to qualified care employment, is not something we have
been able to settle, and we will not pretend otherwise. What is established is
that the restriction is written about recruitment and placement by agencies.
Anyone who tells you flatly that care is closed to Nepalis, or flatly that it is
open, is going further than the evidence goes. If this route matters to you, it
is worth an hour with someone who does immigration law properly.

</div>

## Where to find a family without paying 5 lakh

You do not need an agent in Kathmandu to find a host family, and the embassy
itself expects you to arrive having found one: *"Please only register on the
waiting list once you have actually found a host family and have a valid
contract."*

**The RAL Gütezeichen is the test worth knowing.** It is the German quality mark
for Au Pair agencies, awarded by the Gütegemeinschaft Au pair e.V., set up in
2014 with support from the federal family ministry. Certified agencies are
audited by an outside body on how they prepare, place and support people.

The reason to care is not the badge. It is that **the embassy in Kathmandu
treats a RAL contract differently**: normally you must hand in the original
signed Au Pair contract, but *"for RAL-Au-Pair contracts: copied/scanned version
of the contract is sufficient"*. A German quality mark that saves you posting an
original document across a continent is worth more than any promise an agent
makes you.

**Know which of the two you are using.** An agency places you and supports you,
and charges the host family for it. A platform introduces you and does nothing
else — no agency behind you, and the visa is entirely yours to do. Both are
legitimate. Neither should be charging you 5 lakh.

## What to check before you commit

**The fee.** Au Pair placement is being quoted in Nepal at around 5 lakh. Ask
exactly what that buys. Matching with a family is a real service — and it is
also available free, from the platforms and RAL-certified agencies above. See
[what you should pay](/no-fees/).

**The family.** You will live in their house for a year, at 30 hours a week, in a
country where you know nobody. Speak to them, more than once, before you agree.
Ask to speak to their previous Au Pair.

**That the contract says what it must.** €280 pocket money, the €50 language
course contribution, maximum 30 hours a week, two days off a month. If any of
that is missing from the contract, that is the conversation to have now, not
after you arrive.
