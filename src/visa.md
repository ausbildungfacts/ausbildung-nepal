The residence permit for vocational training is **§ 16a of the Residence Act**
(*Aufenthaltsgesetz*). To apply you need a signed training contract with a
German company, registered with the responsible chamber, and normally proof of
German at **B1**, certified and not older than six months — from one of three
accepted providers: **Goethe-Zentrum Kathmandu**, **VHS (Bhaktapur or Pokhara)**,
or **telc**. You may sit different parts of the exam with different providers,
and the certificate must be submitted **with** the application, not later.
[This matters more than it sounds](/exam-slots/).

Some vocational schools ask for B2 rather than B1 — worth checking with your
employer which school you will attend.

## Why it takes so long from Kathmandu

Since **1 December 2025** the German embassy in Kathmandu no longer lets you
book a visa appointment directly. You enter your name on a waiting list, and the
embassy issues appointments in the order people joined, according to the
capacity it has.

For **Au-Pair and FSJ** visas the embassy publishes an approximate wait of
**8 to 12 months**. We asked whether Ausbildung applicants are treated
differently. The embassy's answer, in August 2026:

> Die Wartelisten treffen alle gleich. Jedoch geht es bei Auszubildenden meist
> etwas schneller als z.B. bei Au-Pairs.

So: the same waiting list applies to you, though trainees usually move through
it somewhat faster than Au-Pairs. The embassy did not give a figure.

Once you do have your appointment, the embassy states that an Ausbildung visa
takes **at least 4 to 6 weeks** to process, because the application has to be
sent to authorities in Germany.

<div class="warn">

**Why this matters more than it sounds.** Berufsschule starts once a year. A
visa that arrives in October does not cost you a few weeks — it can cost you the
entire training year, even though you already have the contract.

</div>

## The accelerated procedure — § 81a

There is a faster route, the *beschleunigtes Fachkräfteverfahren*, and it
**does** apply to vocational training: § 81a covers §§ 16a, 16d, 18a, 18b,
18c(3) and 18g.

Here is the part that matters to you: **you cannot start it.** It is begun by
your future employer, in Germany, at their own local immigration office
(*Ausländerbehörde*).

How it works, according to the official federal description:

- Your employer arranges a first meeting with their Ausländerbehörde and signs
  an agreement with them. **Fee: €411.**
- The Federal Employment Agency's approval counts as given if it raises no
  objection within one week.
- Once the conditions are met, the Ausländerbehörde hands the employer a
  **Vorabzustimmung** (advance approval), which the employer sends to you.
- You state that you hold a Vorabzustimmung when requesting your visa
  appointment. The German mission is then to give you **an appointment within
  three weeks**.
- The decision on the visa should normally follow **within three weeks** of your
  papers being complete.
- The whole procedure is meant to conclude within about two months.

The employer can also authorise a third party to run the procedure for them, so
a company that finds the paperwork daunting does not have to do it alone.

## It is not only paper — one Land publishes its figures

Everything above is what the procedure is *meant* to do. One German state, Lower
Saxony (*Niedersachsen*), publishes what actually happens. Its central office
took in **920 applications** between July 2025 and February 2026, and averaged
**two and a half weeks** from the employer's application to the Vorabzustimmung.

Two cautions before you quote that at anyone. It is one state out of sixteen, and
your employer's office may be quicker or slower — most states publish nothing at
all. And those two and a half weeks are the *German* end of the process. They
come before the three weeks the embassy in Kathmandu takes to give you an
appointment, not instead of them.

Even so, it is the best evidence there is that this procedure moves at the speed
it promises — and it is worth showing to an employer who thinks it sounds too
good to be true.

## Confirmed: Kathmandu honours the three weeks

This is the most important thing on this site, so here it is in the embassy's own
words. We asked the German Embassy in Kathmandu directly whether the three-week
rule is honoured given the current waiting list. Their reply, August 2026:

> Wenn Sie eine Vorabzustimmung erhalten haben, senden Sie uns diese per E-Mail.
> Wir vergeben dann innerhalb der 3-Wochen-Frist einen Termin.

*"If you have received a Vorabzustimmung, send it to us by email. We will then
allocate an appointment within the three-week deadline."*

Two things follow, and neither is published anywhere else we can find.

**The route is real.** An open-ended waiting list becomes a three-week
appointment. That is the difference between arriving for the training year and
missing it.

**You send it to the embassy by email.** Not a form, not a booking system — you
email the Vorabzustimmung to the embassy once your employer has obtained it. The
federal description only says to state that you hold one when booking. Kathmandu
tells you to send it in.

<div class="warn">

**So the single most useful thing you can do is get your employer to start §81a.**
They pay €411 at their own Ausländerbehörde. You cannot do it, and they have
probably never heard of it. Send them this page.

</div>

## Protecting yourself if the visa is slow

Two things are standard practice in Germany, and worth raising with your
employer **before** you sign:

**Ask for the contract to take effect when the visa is issued.** German chambers
advise employers to write the training contract so that it only becomes
effective once the visa has been granted. Then a delay postpones your start
instead of destroying the agreement.

**A start date can be moved.** Before training begins, employer and trainee can
sign an amendment (*Änderungsvereinbarung*) setting a new start date; the end
date shifts too. Training can begin at other points in the year — chambers hold
two examination sittings annually. Backdating a contract is not permitted, but
postponing one is ordinary.

German chambers advise employers to allow **4 to 6 months** between signing the
contract and entry, and to recruit **6 to 8 months** before the intended start.
If your employer is working to a shorter timetable, they may not know how long
this takes from Nepal.
